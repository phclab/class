Deep brain stimulation treats Parkinson's disease, essential tremor and dystonia by changing a circuit, not a single nucleus. Below is a live model of the two motor loops involved: the basal ganglia loop, which loses its dopamine in Parkinson's disease, and the cerebello-thalamo-cortical loop behind tremor. What goes wrong in Parkinson's disease

The nigrostriatal pathway runs from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) in the midbrain to the dorsal striatum, mostly the putamen. In Parkinson's disease these dopamine neurons degenerate, with Lewy bodies made of aggregated α-synuclein in the survivors. The motor signs — slowness (bradykinesia), rigidity, a 4–6 Hz tremor at rest and, later, unstable posture — usually appear only once roughly 60–80% of striatal dopamine is gone. The terminals seem to go first and the cell bodies later, so only about a third to a half of the neurons are lost at diagnosis.

Dopamine itself does not move anything. It sets the balance between two populations of striatal projection neurons, and the rest of the loop amplifies that imbalance.

Direct pathway: D1 → GPi

D1 receptors couple to Gαs/olf, raise cAMP and make these neurons easier to drive. They inhibit GPi/SNr directly. GPi is itself inhibitory and fires tonically at 60–80 Hz, holding the motor thalamus down; when D1 neurons fire, GPi pauses and the thalamus is released. Net effect: go.

Indirect pathway: D2 → GPe → STN → GPi

D2 receptors couple to Gαi/o and make these neurons harder to drive. They inhibit GPe, which releases the subthalamic nucleus, whose glutamate excites GPi. Two inhibitions and an excitation: the net effect is more GPi, a firmer brake. Net effect: no-go.

Because dopamine excites one pathway and suppresses the other, losing it pushes both in the same direction: the direct pathway falls silent, the indirect pathway becomes overactive, and both converge on more GPi output and less thalamic drive to motor cortex. This is the rate model of Albin, Young and Penney (1989) and DeLong (1990). It predicted, correctly, that silencing the subthalamic nucleus should relieve parkinsonism in monkeys made parkinsonian with MPTP (Bergman, Wichmann and DeLong, 1990) — the origin of today's STN deep brain stimulation.

Rates are half the story: the beta rhythm

The rate model cannot explain everything. Lesioning GPi helps, but so does adding more stimulation to STN, and some patients improve without their firing rates returning to normal. What recordings from patients show instead is a change in pattern: neurons in STN and GPe start firing in synchrony, and the local field potential carries a strong oscillation at 13–30 Hz, the beta band. Beta power falls with levodopa and with DBS roughly in proportion to how much bradykinesia and rigidity improve, which is why adaptive DBS systems now use STN beta as their feedback signal.

The rhythm comes from the STN–GPe loop: STN excites GPe, GPe inhibits STN, and each step takes a few milliseconds. A loop with negative feedback and delay oscillates once its gain is high enough (Holgado, Terry and Bogacz, 2010). After dopamine loss, GPe→STN synapses proliferate (Fan et al., 2012), raising that gain. In this model the GPe→STN weight grows with the dopamine deficit, and the loop starts to oscillate at about 16 Hz once around 70% of SNc output is gone. Watch the STN and GPe lanes above while you drag the SNc slider down.

Tremor is a different rhythm, slower and generated in a different loop — see the next section.

A second loop: the cerebellum, Vim and tremor

The motor thalamus is not one relay. Its front part, VA/VL, receives the GABAergic output of GPi and projects mainly to premotor cortex and the SMA: that is the basal ganglia loop above. Just behind it, the ventral intermediate nucleus (Vim) receives excitatory input from the dentate nucleus of the cerebellum and projects mainly to primary motor cortex. M1 sends a copy of every command through the pontine nuclei to the cerebellar cortex, whose Purkinje cells inhibit the dentate nucleus, which drives Vim, which drives M1 again.

That cerebello-thalamo-cortical loop contains one inhibitory step, so it is a negative feedback loop with delay. Such a loop is stable while its gain is low and starts to oscillate once the gain is too high; here the rhythm comes out at about 7 Hz, inside the 4–12 Hz range of essential tremor. Post-mortem studies of essential tremor find Purkinje cell damage and fewer GABA receptors in the dentate nucleus, both of which would leave the dentate under-inhibited. A second idea comes from the harmaline model of essential tremor: the inferior olive, which drives Purkinje cells through climbing fibres, starts to oscillate at 6–10 Hz. The Essential tremor preset combines both — a 7 Hz olivary drive into the Purkinje cells and a dentate 30% stronger than normal — so the loop resonates with a rhythm that it did not make itself. That distinction matters for stimulation: high-frequency Vim DBS stops the rhythm from reaching M1, while 20 Hz stimulation only adds to it, as Benabid and colleagues found when they first tested frequencies in the 1980s.

This is why thalamic DBS for tremor goes to Vim and not to VA/VL. Stimulating Vim replaces its output with a steady signal, the loop opens and the oscillation stops, but the basal ganglia brake on VA/VL is untouched: in Parkinson's disease, Vim DBS can abolish the tremor and leave slowness and rigidity exactly where they were. STN and GPi stimulation help Parkinsonian tremor too, which led to the dimmer–switch idea (Helmich et al., 2012): the basal ganglia switch tremor episodes on, and the cerebellar loop sets their amplitude. That trigger is not in this model, so the Parkinson's presets here have no tremor.

Dystonia: a weak brake, in the wrong rhythm

Dystonia is a movement disorder of sustained or intermittent muscle contractions that twist the body into abnormal, often repetitive postures — in one region, such as the neck or the hand that writes, or across the whole body, as in the inherited TOR1A (DYT1) form. It is a disorder of a network rather than of one nucleus: patients show reduced inhibition inside motor cortex, abnormal plasticity at corticostriatal synapses and fewer available D2 receptors, and the cerebellum is involved too.

Recordings made during DBS surgery show the pallidal side of it. GPi neurons fire lower than in Parkinson's disease, and irregularly, in bursts and pauses; the local field potential carries a strong 4–12 Hz rhythm that is coherent with the dystonic muscle activity. The Dystonia preset reproduces that picture: the direct pathway is stronger and the indirect pathway weaker, so GPi falls and the thalamus is released, and a synchronised 6 Hz rhythm is injected into the striatum. Where that rhythm really comes from is unknown — striatal cholinergic interneurons, cortex and cerebellum are all candidates — so the model does not pretend to generate it.

GPi DBS is the standard treatment, and it shows the paradox of pallidal surgery most clearly. GPi is already too low, yet stimulating it helps: high-frequency stimulation replaces its bursts and pauses with a steady signal, and the thalamus stops following the rhythm. STN is used as an alternative target. Unlike tremor, which stops within seconds, dystonia improves over weeks to months after the stimulator is switched on, a sign that slow plasticity in the network does much of the work; that is not in this model.

What to try

  1. From Healthy, drag the SNc slider down slowly. GPi rises and the thalamus falls well before anything oscillates; beta appears only near 30% remaining. Clinical symptoms also wait for a large loss.
  2. At Parkinson's, press Send a movement command. In a healthy circuit the command releases the thalamus; here the silent direct pathway cannot pass it, and the overactive indirect and hyperdirect pathways push GPi up instead.
  3. Add levodopa a little at a time. Around 80% added (back to 100% total) everything is normal; a little more and GPi collapses — the circuit version of peak-dose dyskinesia.
  4. Start from Parkinson's and click each region of the circuit in turn to stimulate it. Which ones help, and which make things worse? With the electrode in STN, lower the amplitude: how much stimulation does it take to stop the beta rhythm?
  5. Keep the STN electrode and switch between HFS and LFS. Which one removes the beta rhythm, and which one imposes a rhythm of its own?
  6. Put the electrode in GPi, then switch the disease between Parkinson's and STN stroke. GPi is too high in the first and too low in the second, yet the same stimulation helps both. Why?
  7. With an STN or GPi electrode, press Send a movement command. The command no longer passes through the stimulated output — what does the thalamus do instead?
  8. At Parkinson's, compare STN DBS with the pallidotomy in the treatment table. Both relieve the thalamus; only DBS stops the beta rhythm.
  9. Press Essential tremor and watch the M1 lane. Then try every DBS target. Only one stops the tremor without touching the basal ganglia. Now press Parkinson's and put DBS in Vim: what happens to the VA/VL thalamus?
  10. From Healthy, raise the dentate slider slowly. At what strength does the loop start to oscillate? Does lowering any other node in the same loop (pons, Purkinje, Vim, M1) bring it back?
  11. Press Dystonia and watch the GPi and VA/VL lanes: low, and pulsing at 6 Hz. Put the electrode in GPi with HFS. Which number changes more — the mean rate or the rhythm? Then try Vim.
  12. Weaken one node at a time in a healthy circuit and predict the sign of the change at GPi before you look. STN stroke and a weak D2 slider (loss of indirect-pathway neurons) both release the thalamus: involuntary movement is the mirror image of parkinsonism.

What this model leaves out
  • Spikes and individual neurons. Each nucleus is one average rate, so the model shows synchrony only as an oscillation of that average.
  • Parallel channels. Real basal ganglia keep many action channels side by side; selecting one movement means releasing one channel while braking the rest. Recordings also show direct and indirect pathway neurons active together at movement onset, which the simple go/no-go picture does not capture.
  • Striatal interneurons (cholinergic, fast-spiking), the SNr–GPi distinction, the pedunculopontine nucleus and thalamostriatal projections.
  • Plasticity. Dopamine also teaches corticostriatal synapses; that learning, and the brain's compensation before symptoms appear, is absent.
  • Drug kinetics. Levodopa here is a steady level, not a dose that rises and wears off.
  • Parts of how DBS works. The six mechanisms above are reduced to rates. Left out: cortex has no beta of its own here, so jamming the hyperdirect axons does little; partial axonal conduction failure; the side effects of current spreading into the internal capsule, medial lemniscus or oculomotor fibres; electrode geometry and directional leads; and the plasticity that makes dystonia improve over weeks.
  • The origin of the essential tremor rhythm. The inferior olive is represented by a fixed 7 Hz drive, not by its own neurons.
  • The origin of the dystonic rhythm. It is injected into the striatum as a fixed 6 Hz drive; the model does not explain where it comes from or why dystonia improves slowly after surgery.
  • Parkinsonian rest tremor. The cerebellar loop can oscillate here, but the basal ganglia trigger of the dimmer–switch model is absent, and so are the inferior olive and climbing fibres, the other proposed tremor generator.
  • Non-motor symptoms (smell, sleep, mood, autonomic) and the parts of the disease outside the nigrostriatal pathway.
