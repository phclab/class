Deep Brain Stimulation at the Synapse

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU · LinkedIn

Deep brain stimulation fires the axons near the electrode 130 times a second or more — far faster than a synapse can refill. Within a few pulses the terminal runs out of vesicles ready to release, and every later pulse, including the neuron's own spikes, finds almost nothing to release. Below is one axon terminal from the subthalamic nucleus onto a GPi neuron, slowed down so you can watch it happen.

Why the vesicles run out

An action potential does not empty a terminal. It opens calcium channels at the active zone, and each vesicle already docked and primed there fuses with some probability — here 35%. That docked set, the readily releasable pool, is small: a dozen sites in this terminal. Behind it sits a larger reserve pool, and after fusion the membrane is taken back by endocytosis and refilled with transmitter, which takes on the order of a second or more.

Refilling an empty docking site takes time too — a few hundred milliseconds here. Between two spikes 50 ms apart (20 Hz) a good fraction of the sites refill. Between two DBS pulses 7.7 ms apart (130 Hz) almost none do. So the first pulse of a train releases about four vesicles, and within ten or twenty pulses each pulse releases a fraction of one: the terminal can only release vesicles as fast as it can dock new ones.

The total release rate therefore hits a ceiling. Doubling the stimulation frequency does not double the transmitter delivered; it divides the same trickle among twice as many pulses. Meanwhile the trickle keeps drawing on the reserve faster than recycling returns vesicles to it, so the reserve shrinks too.

Why that helps in Parkinson's disease

In Parkinson's disease, STN neurons fire in bursts locked to the beta rhythm (13–30 Hz), and the synapse onto GPi faithfully passes that rhythm on. Turn on the orange spikes with DBS off: each one releases a couple of vesicles, and the postsynaptic trace bumps with every burst.

Now switch DBS to 130 Hz. The DBS pulses and the neuron's own spikes use the same terminal and the same docked vesicles. The pool is kept nearly empty, so each of the neuron's own spikes now releases a fraction of a vesicle, and the postsynaptic trace becomes a flat, steady trickle that no longer carries the beta pattern. The pathological rhythm is still generated upstream, but it can no longer get through. This is one reason high-frequency stimulation acts like a reversible lesion of the stimulated pathway — and why, in the DBS Explorer, DBS is modelled as taking away a nucleus' patterned output.

The same arithmetic explains why frequency matters clinically. Stimulation at 5–20 Hz does not deplete the terminal; it releases a full complement of transmitter on every pulse, can entrain the circuit, and tends to worsen slowness rather than relieve it. Benefit appears above roughly 100 Hz, where each pulse releases little.

What to try

  1. With DBS off and the orange spikes on, watch the docked row: sites empty with each burst and mostly refill before the next. Note the release per own spike.
  2. Press Refill all vesicles, then switch to 130 Hz at 1/50 speed. Count the vesicles released by the first five pulses. How many pulses until the docked row stays empty?
  3. Compare 20, 60, 130 and 185 Hz after a few seconds of model time. Release per pulse falls with frequency, but the release rate per second barely changes. Why?
  4. At 130 Hz, compare the release per own spike with the number from step 1. That ratio is how strongly DBS filters the beta signal at this synapse.
  5. Turn DBS off and watch the reserve pool and the docked row recover. Recovery takes longer than depletion did.
The model and its numbers

Every vesicle is simulated individually, so the counts are random from pulse to pulse, as they are at a real synapse. Time runs in steps of 0.25 ms of model time; the playback speed only changes how fast you watch it.

on each spike: every docked vesicle fuses with probability p = 0.35 empty site refills from the reserve: rate 1/τ_dock, τ_dock = 250 ms fused vesicle returns to the reserve after an exponential delay, mean τ_recycle = 1.5 s 12 docking sites, 80 reserve vesicles postsynaptic current: +1 per vesicle, decays with τ = 4 ms

The average behaviour is the depressing synapse of Tsodyks and Markram (1997). If x is the fraction of docked sites filled and spikes come every T ms, the steady state is

xss = (1 − e−T/τ) / (1 − (1 − p) e−T/τ) release per spike = p · N · xss (N = 12, ignoring the reserve)

Real synapses differ by an order of magnitude in each of these numbers, and many recover faster or slower; the qualitative result — release per pulse collapses once the interval is much shorter than the refilling time — holds wherever the pool is small.

What else DBS does, and what this page leaves out
  • Evidence. Recordings in patients during surgery show responses evoked by stimulation collapsing within a few pulses at high frequencies but not at low ones (Milosevic et al., 2018), and models that combine synaptic depression with axonal failure reproduce the loss of oscillation transfer (Rosenbaum et al., 2014).
  • Axonal failure. At high frequency some axons stop conducting every pulse, which adds to the filtering shown here.
  • Somata versus axons. DBS excites axons more easily than cell bodies. Near the electrode, cell bodies can be silenced by stimulated inhibitory inputs while their axons are driven at the stimulation frequency; spikes also travel backwards (antidromically) and collide with the neuron's own.
  • Glia. Astrocytes near the electrode release ATP and adenosine during stimulation, which can depress transmission further.
  • Inhibitory synapses. The same depletion happens at GABAergic terminals, which matters for GPi stimulation, where most stimulated afferents are inhibitory.
  • This page shows one terminal. It does not include short-term facilitation, the calcium dependence of refilling, or the separate fates of the many axon branches one neuron makes.
