Hodgkin–Huxley Model Explorer

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU · LinkedIn

Four equations, written in 1952 from voltage-clamp measurements on the squid giant axon, are enough to produce an action potential from nothing but ions crossing a membrane. Inject a current below and watch the sodium gates open, the potassium gates follow, and the spike build and terminate itself.

m is so fast that it can follow the voltage instantly, which removes one equation. h and n then turn out to move in step — h falls roughly as n rises — which removes another; that is an empirical fit, not a consequence of the speeds. What is left is a plane, and the spike becomes a loop around it. The curves are the nullclines — the places where V, or n, stops changing. They cross at the resting state, bottom left.

What m, h and n actually are

Hodgkin and Huxley could not see a channel, so they described one with gating particles. Each particle is either open or closed. It opens at a rate α and closes at a rate β, and both rates depend only on voltage. Hold the membrane at a voltage and the population settles where the two flows balance.

x∞ = α / (α + β)   ·   τ = 1 / (α + β)   ·   x(t) = x∞ + (x0 − x∞) e−t/τ

Each square flips on its own, at the rates shown above. No single particle knows what fraction is open; the fraction is just what a large number of independent coin flips settles at. That is the whole content of x∞, and τ is how long the crowd takes to get there after you change the voltage.

Why m³h and n⁴

A sodium channel conducts only when all three of its m particles are open and its h particle has not closed, so the open probability is m³h. Hodgkin and Huxley meant particles in the membrane, not a channel — no one had seen one — and the assumption that m and h act independently is one the real protein only roughly honours. Potassium needs four n particles, giving n⁴. The powers were chosen to fit the S-shaped onset of the measured currents: one particle gives an exponential rise, several that must all be open at once give the delay that the data showed.

Why the spike works

Look at the middle panel. For m, τ is under a millisecond everywhere, so m is always nearly at x∞ — it tracks the voltage. For h and n, τ is several milliseconds near rest, so they lag. Depolarisation therefore opens m immediately, and only later closes h and opens n. That lag is the action potential.

How m, h and n are actually computed

They are not solved for. They are integrated, one small step at a time, and the voltage and the gates take turns feeding each other. The step here is 5 µs.

Start at rest. Each gate begins at the value it would settle to if the voltage were held at the model's resting potential:

m = αm(V₀) / (αm(V₀) + βm(V₀)) and the same for h and n

Then, every step, move the voltage using the present gates:

iNa = ḡNa · m³h · (V − ENa) iK = ḡK · n⁴ · (V − EK) iL = ḡL · (V − EL) V ← V + dt · (I − iNa − iK − iL) / Cm

Then move the gates using the new voltage:

τ = 1 / (φ · (α(V) + β(V))) x∞ = α(V) / (α(V) + β(V)) x ← x∞ + (x − x∞) · e−dt/τ

That last line is the exact solution of dx/dt = (x∞ − x)/τ for a voltage held constant across the step, not a difference approximation. It matters: in the fast-spiking model at 37 °C τm is about 0.03 ms, far shorter than some of the steps a browser would otherwise take, and a plain forward step would blow up where this one cannot.

Then back to the voltage, with the new gates. Nothing in the loop knows what an action potential is; the spike is what the loop does.

Two details worth knowing. In the fast-spiking model m is not integrated at all — it is set to m∞(V) every step, because Wang and Buzsáki treated sodium activation as instantaneous. And φ multiplies only the gate rates, never the voltage equation: warming the model speeds the gates while leaving the membrane's charging time alone, which is exactly why the squid set stops firing near body temperature.

Accuracy: against a fourth-order Runge–Kutta reference at a tenth of the step, the spike peak here differs by 0.11 mV for the squid set, 0.34 mV for the cortical set and 0.14 mV for the fast-spiking set, and the spike width by under one per cent.

Where those numbers came from

None of the constants in those rate equations was derived. Hodgkin and Huxley measured them, in a series of papers published in 1952, on the giant axon of the squid Loligo forbesi at Plymouth. The axon is 400–800 µm across, wide enough to thread electrodes down its middle: two fine silver wires wound on a glass capillary and pushed twenty or thirty millimetres along the axis, one to pass current and one to measure voltage, in a method taken from Cole and from Marmont. Those internal wires are what make the rest possible: it short-circuits the inside so the whole length of membrane sits at one potential, and a feedback amplifier then holds that potential wherever the experimenter puts it and reports the current needed to keep it there. Without the clamp, voltage and conductance chase each other and neither can be measured; with it, voltage is the knob and current is the reading.

The raw record. Step the membrane from rest to, say, 0 mV and hold it. The current shows three things in succession: a brief capacitive surge as the membrane charges, then an inward current that grows and dies away within a few milliseconds although the voltage is still being held, then an outward current that rises with a delay and stays up until the step ends. Everything that follows is an attempt to account for those two later components.

  1. Separate the two currents. Replace the sodium in the seawater with choline, an inert cation. The axon stops being excitable but holds its resting potential, and the inward hump vanishes — not because sodium has stopped moving, but because with no sodium outside it can only move outward, so the record now shows an early outward hump instead. Comparing the two records is what separates the currents: assume potassium behaves identically in both solutions and that sodium only changes in size and sign, and the sodium current falls out of the difference, leaving potassium as the remainder. Neither current was ever recorded on its own.
  2. Turn current into conductance. A current is not a property of the membrane — it depends on how far the voltage sits from the ion's equilibrium potential. Divide by that driving force, g = I / (V − E), taking ENa from the step at which the transient reverses direction. Now each clamp step has given two curves against time: gK(t), which rises with an S-shaped delay and stays up, and gNa(t), which rises faster and then falls while the voltage is still held. That decline is what inactivation means, and it is why sodium needs two variables and potassium only one.
  3. Straighten the potassium curve. A delayed S-shaped rise is what several independent particles that must all be open produce. Raise gK(t)/gK,∞ to the power of one quarter and the S-shape flattens into a simple exponential approach to its final level. The fourth power was a convenience, not a result: Hodgkin and Huxley wrote that a fifth or sixth power would have fitted slightly better, but not well enough to be worth the extra complication. The same trick with a cube root does the equivalent job for the rising phase of sodium, which is where m³ comes from.
  4. Read off two numbers, convert to two rates. The final level gives n∞ and the time to cover 63 per cent of the change gives τ. Then α = n∞/τ and β = (1 − n∞)/τ. One clamp step yields one pair of rate constants, at one voltage. Inactivation needs a second protocol: hold a conditioning voltage, then step to a fixed test voltage and see how much sodium current survives — that gives h∞(V), and the recovery between paired steps gives τh.
  5. Repeat, plot, and draw a curve. Do it at a dozen or more voltages on one axon, pool the points from several axons, plot α and β against voltage, and draw smooth functions through them. Those functions — chosen to fit, not deduced — are the equations at the top of this page.

Why the 1952 equations look different from the ones above

Hodgkin and Huxley did not write voltage as we do. Theirs was the displacement from the resting potential, with the sign reversed, so depolarisation was negative. In their notation:

αm = 0.1(V + 25)/(e(V+25)/10 − 1) βm = 4 eV/18 αh = 0.07 eV/20 βh = 1/(e(V+30)/10 + 1) αn = 0.01(V + 10)/(e(V+10)/10 − 1) βn = 0.125 eV/80

Substitute V = −(Vm + 65) and every one of them turns into the modern form printed at the top of this page — the two sets agree to the last digit at every voltage. So the 65 that appears everywhere is not a measurement at all: it is the squid axon's resting potential, carried in by the change of variable. The offsets follow from it too: 40 = 65 − 25, 55 = 65 − 10, 35 = 65 − 30.

What was actually fitted is the rest: the amplitudes 0.1, 4, 0.07, 0.125, 0.01, the offsets 25, 10, 30, and the slope factors 10, 18, 20, 80 millivolts per e-fold. The exponential shapes were not arbitrary — a charged particle sitting in the membrane field should distribute exponentially with voltage — but the numbers came off the graphs.

Getting m∞ out, even though sodium inactivates

The peak of gNa is already too small: h starts falling before m has finished rising, so no record ever shows what sodium would have done on its own. The way round it is to use the decay. By then m is steady, so the fall is h alone — fit it with an exponential and carry that exponential back to the start of the step. The intercept is the conductance there would have been without inactivation. Divide by ḡNa and by h at the holding potential, take the cube root, and you have m∞ at that command potential. Hold near −120 mV and h ≈ 1, so the second correction almost vanishes.

Running the procedure on the model returns the constants the model was built from, which is only a consistency check. The real work was doing it on an axon, in 1952, with records photographed off an oscilloscope, curves fitted to the data by hand, and the equations then integrated on a hand-cranked calculator over about three weeks — and then showing that the resulting equations, integrated numerically, produced an action potential nobody had put into them.

What to try

  • Find threshold. Drop the current until the spike disappears. Just below it the membrane makes a small bump and returns; just above it the full spike appears. Almost nothing in between: the band of half-sized responses is about a hundredth of a µA/cm² wide, far too narrow to find by hand, which is what all-or-none means in practice. The readout shows the smallest current that still fires.
  • Refractory period. Switch to two pulses and shorten the interval. Below about 10 ms the second pulse produces nothing at all, because h is still closed: this is the absolute refractory period. For a few milliseconds beyond that it fires but needs more current and the spike is smaller — relative refractoriness, set by h recovering and n still open. Raise the current and both windows shorten; this squid model at 6.3 °C is far slower than a mammalian neuron at body temperature.
  • Repetitive firing. Switch to sustained current and raise the amplitude. The axon fires a train whose rate rises with current, but only over a limited range — the spike is not a rate code with unlimited bandwidth.
  • Block the channels. TTX removes the sodium current and the spike with it; TEA removes the potassium current and the spike becomes long and broad, because repolarisation now depends on leak and on sodium inactivation alone.
  • Watch the loop close. In the phase plane a subthreshold stimulus pushes the state a little to the right and it slides straight back down the left branch of the V-nullcline. A suprathreshold one carries it past the middle branch, and there is no way back except the long way round — up the right branch, across the top and down again. That detour is the action potential, and the middle branch is what acts as the threshold here — the line the stimulus has to clear.
  • Hold a voltage and watch the particles. In the gating panel, step the holding potential from −65 mV to 0 mV. The m squares flip open almost at once; the h squares empty slowly; n fills slowly. Those three time constants, read off the middle graph, are all you need to understand why the spike rises, stops and repolarises in that order.
  • Swap the neuron. The squid equations are the original, but no mammalian cell works that way. The cortical set (Traub and Miles) and the fast-spiking interneuron set (Wang and Buzsáki) use faster gates, a more negative potassium reversal potential and, in the interneuron, sodium activation so fast that m is treated as instantaneous. Compare the spike widths under a sustained current: both mammalian sets fire far faster and far more briefly than the squid axon. Neither accommodates, because these reduced sets carry no adaptation currents — exactly what a real pyramidal cell adds and a fast-spiking interneuron largely does without.
  • Cool it down. Hodgkin and Huxley recorded between about 3 and 11 °C, with a few runs far warmer, and reduced everything to a standard 6.3 °C. Raise the temperature and every rate constant speeds up by a factor of about 3 per 10 °C, so the spike gets narrower and faster; at mammalian temperature this squid model stops firing altogether, which is why warm-blooded axons need different channel kinetics.

How the spike builds and stops itself

The regenerative loop

Depolarisation opens m gates, sodium enters, the membrane depolarises further and opens more m gates. That positive feedback is why the upstroke needs no further help once it starts, and why there is a threshold: below it, leak and potassium current win; above it, the loop runs away.

Two brakes, on a delay

The same depolarisation slowly closes h, which inactivates the sodium channels, and slowly opens n, which lets potassium out. Both are too slow to stop the upstroke but arrive in time to end it. The membrane then overshoots below rest, because potassium conductance is still high — the afterhyperpolarisation.

m is fast and h and n are slow: that single separation of timescales is the whole mechanism. Take it away — make h as fast as m — and the model no longer spikes.

The squid model does not simply scale to body temperature: raise its temperature towards 37 °C and the spike first narrows, then fails altogether, because the gates speed up while the membrane's charging time does not: inactivation and potassium now outrun the sodium current before it can charge the capacitance. Mammalian channels solve this with different kinetics, not with a different temperature coefficient — which is why a separate parameter set is needed rather than a warmer squid.
