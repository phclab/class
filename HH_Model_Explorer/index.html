Hodgkin–Huxley Model Explorer

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU · LinkedIn

Four equations, written in 1952 from voltage-clamp measurements on the squid giant axon, are enough to produce an action potential from nothing but ions crossing a membrane. Inject a current below and watch the sodium gates open, the potassium gates follow, and the spike build and terminate itself.

Because m is so much faster than h and n, the four equations can be squeezed into two: let m follow the voltage instantly and let h fall as n rises. What is left is a plane, and the spike becomes a loop around it. The curves are the nullclines — the places where V, or n, stops changing. They cross at the resting state, bottom left.

What to try

  • Find threshold. Drop the current until the spike disappears. Just below it the membrane makes a small bump and returns; just above it the full spike appears. Nothing in between — that is what all-or-none means, and the readout shows the smallest current that still fires.
  • Refractory period. Switch to two pulses and shorten the interval. Below about 3 ms the second pulse produces nothing at all, because h is still closed: this is the absolute refractory period. Between roughly 3 and 12 ms it fires but needs more current and the spike is smaller — relative refractoriness, set by h recovering and n still open.
  • Repetitive firing. Switch to sustained current and raise the amplitude. The axon fires a train whose rate rises with current, but only over a limited range — the spike is not a rate code with unlimited bandwidth.
  • Block the channels. TTX removes the sodium current and the spike with it; TEA removes the potassium current and the spike becomes long and broad, because repolarisation now depends on leak and on sodium inactivation alone.
  • Watch the loop close. In the phase plane a subthreshold stimulus pushes the state a little to the right and it slides straight back down the left branch of the V-nullcline. A suprathreshold one carries it past the middle branch, and there is no way back except the long way round — up the right branch, across the top and down again. That detour is the action potential, and the middle branch is what "threshold" really means.
  • Swap the neuron. The squid equations are the original, but no mammalian cell works that way. The cortical set (Traub and Miles) and the fast-spiking interneuron set (Wang and Buzsáki) use faster gates, a more negative potassium reversal potential and, in the interneuron, sodium activation so fast that m is treated as instantaneous. Compare the spike widths and the firing rates under a sustained current: the interneuron is built to fire hundreds of hertz without accommodating.
  • Cool it down. Hodgkin and Huxley worked at 6.3 °C. Raise the temperature and every rate constant speeds up by a factor of about 3 per 10 °C, so the spike gets narrower and faster; at mammalian temperature this squid model stops firing altogether, which is why warm-blooded axons need different channel kinetics.

How the spike builds and stops itself

The regenerative loop

Depolarisation opens m gates, sodium enters, the membrane depolarises further and opens more m gates. That positive feedback is why the upstroke needs no further help once it starts, and why there is a threshold: below it, leak and potassium current win; above it, the loop runs away.

Two brakes, on a delay

The same depolarisation slowly closes h, which inactivates the sodium channels, and slowly opens n, which lets potassium out. Both are too slow to stop the upstroke but arrive in time to end it. The membrane then overshoots below rest, because potassium conductance is still high — the afterhyperpolarisation.

m is fast and h and n are slow: that single separation of timescales is the whole mechanism. Take it away — make h as fast as m — and the model no longer spikes.

The equations

αm = 0.1(V + 40) / (1 − e−(V+40)/10)    βm = 4 e−(V+65)/18
αh = 0.07 e−(V+65)/20    βh = 1 / (1 + e−(V+35)/10)
αn = 0.01(V + 55) / (1 − e−(V+55)/10)    βn = 0.125 e−(V+65)/80
rates × φ, with φ = 3(T − 6.3)/10

The exponents are not theory. Hodgkin and Huxley chose m³h and n⁴ because those powers fitted the sigmoid onset of the measured currents; the interpretation as independent gating particles came afterwards, and the modern reading is that they approximate the sequential conformational changes of the channel protein.

The squid model does not simply scale to body temperature: raise its temperature towards 37 °C and the spike first narrows, then fails altogether, because every rate speeds up by the same factor and sodium inactivation catches the upstroke before it can regenerate. Mammalian channels solve this with different kinetics, not with a different temperature coefficient — which is why a separate parameter set is needed rather than a warmer squid.
