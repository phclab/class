m is so fast that it can follow the voltage instantly, which removes one equation. h and n then turn out to move in step — h falls roughly as n rises — which removes another; that is an empirical fit, not a consequence of the speeds. What is left is a plane, and the spike becomes a loop around it. The curves are the nullclines — the places where V, or n, stops changing. They cross at the resting state, bottom left.

What m, h and n actually are

Hodgkin and Huxley could not see a channel, so they described one with gating particles. Each particle is either open or closed. It opens at a rate α and closes at a rate β, and both rates depend only on voltage. Hold the membrane at a voltage and the population settles where the two flows balance.

Each square flips on its own, at the rates shown above. No single particle knows what fraction is open; the fraction is just what a large number of independent coin flips settles at. That is the whole content of x∞, and τ is how long the crowd takes to get there after you change the voltage.

Why m³h and n⁴

A sodium channel conducts only when all three of its m particles are open and its h particle has not closed, so the open probability is m³h. Hodgkin and Huxley meant particles in the membrane, not a channel — no one had seen one — and the assumption that m and h act independently is one the real protein only roughly honours. Potassium needs four n particles, giving n⁴. The powers were chosen to fit the S-shaped onset of the measured currents: one particle gives an exponential rise, several that must all be open at once give the delay that the data showed.

Why the spike works

Look at the middle panel. For m, τ is under a millisecond everywhere, so m is always nearly at x∞ — it tracks the voltage. For h and n, τ is several milliseconds near rest, so they lag. Depolarisation therefore opens m immediately, and only later closes h and opens n. That lag is the action potential.

Where those numbers came from

None of the constants in those rate equations was derived. Hodgkin and Huxley measured them. The membrane was held at a fixed voltage with a feedback amplifier — the voltage clamp — so that the only thing changing was the conductance. Sodium and potassium currents were separated by replacing the sodium in the seawater with choline: what remained was the potassium current, and the difference gave sodium. Dividing each current by its driving force gave a conductance, and the shape of that conductance over time is what the gating variables were invented to describe.

  1. Step the voltage and record. Potassium conductance rises with a delay and stays up; sodium conductance rises faster, then falls even though the voltage is still held — that decline is what inactivation means.
  2. Take the fourth root. A delayed, S-shaped rise is exactly what four independent particles in series produce. Plot gK(t)/gK,∞ to the power of one quarter and the S-shape straightens into a single exponential.
  3. Read off the two numbers. The final level gives n∞, and the time to cover 63 per cent of the distance from the starting level to that final level gives τ.
  4. Convert. α = n∞/τ and β = (1 − n∞)/τ. One clamp step yields one pair of rate constants, at one voltage.
  5. Repeat and fit. Do it at twenty voltages, plot α and β against voltage, and draw smooth curves through the points. Those curves — chosen to fit, not deduced — are the equations at the top of this page.

Running the procedure on the model returns the constants the model was built from, which is only a consistency check. The real work was doing it on an axon, in 1952, with records photographed off an oscilloscope, curves fitted to the data by hand, and the equations then integrated on a hand-cranked calculator over about three weeks — and then showing that the resulting equations, integrated numerically, produced an action potential nobody had put into them.

What to try

  • Find threshold. Drop the current until the spike disappears. Just below it the membrane makes a small bump and returns; just above it the full spike appears. Almost nothing in between: the band of half-sized responses is about a hundredth of a µA/cm² wide, far too narrow to find by hand, which is what all-or-none means in practice. The readout shows the smallest current that still fires.
  • Refractory period. Switch to two pulses and shorten the interval. Below about 10 ms the second pulse produces nothing at all, because h is still closed: this is the absolute refractory period. For a few milliseconds beyond that it fires but needs more current and the spike is smaller — relative refractoriness, set by h recovering and n still open. Raise the current and both windows shorten; this squid model at 6.3 °C is far slower than a mammalian neuron at body temperature.
  • Repetitive firing. Switch to sustained current and raise the amplitude. The axon fires a train whose rate rises with current, but only over a limited range — the spike is not a rate code with unlimited bandwidth.
  • Block the channels. TTX removes the sodium current and the spike with it; TEA removes the potassium current and the spike becomes long and broad, because repolarisation now depends on leak and on sodium inactivation alone.
  • Watch the loop close. In the phase plane a subthreshold stimulus pushes the state a little to the right and it slides straight back down the left branch of the V-nullcline. A suprathreshold one carries it past the middle branch, and there is no way back except the long way round — up the right branch, across the top and down again. That detour is the action potential, and the middle branch is what acts as the threshold here — the line the stimulus has to clear.
  • Hold a voltage and watch the particles. In the gating panel, step the holding potential from −65 mV to 0 mV. The m squares flip open almost at once; the h squares empty slowly; n fills slowly. Those three time constants, read off the middle graph, are all you need to understand why the spike rises, stops and repolarises in that order.
  • Swap the neuron. The squid equations are the original, but no mammalian cell works that way. The cortical set (Traub and Miles) and the fast-spiking interneuron set (Wang and Buzsáki) use faster gates, a more negative potassium reversal potential and, in the interneuron, sodium activation so fast that m is treated as instantaneous. Compare the spike widths under a sustained current: both mammalian sets fire far faster and far more briefly than the squid axon. Neither accommodates, because these reduced sets carry no adaptation currents — exactly what a real pyramidal cell adds and a fast-spiking interneuron largely does without.
  • Cool it down. Hodgkin and Huxley worked at 6.3 °C. Raise the temperature and every rate constant speeds up by a factor of about 3 per 10 °C, so the spike gets narrower and faster; at mammalian temperature this squid model stops firing altogether, which is why warm-blooded axons need different channel kinetics.

How the spike builds and stops itself

The regenerative loop

Depolarisation opens m gates, sodium enters, the membrane depolarises further and opens more m gates. That positive feedback is why the upstroke needs no further help once it starts, and why there is a threshold: below it, leak and potassium current win; above it, the loop runs away.

Two brakes, on a delay

The same depolarisation slowly closes h, which inactivates the sodium channels, and slowly opens n, which lets potassium out. Both are too slow to stop the upstroke but arrive in time to end it. The membrane then overshoots below rest, because potassium conductance is still high — the afterhyperpolarisation.

m is fast and h and n are slow: that single separation of timescales is the whole mechanism. Take it away — make h as fast as m — and the model no longer spikes.

The exponents are not theory. Hodgkin and Huxley chose m³h and n⁴ because those powers fitted the sigmoid onset of the measured currents; the interpretation as independent gating particles came afterwards, and the modern reading is that they approximate the sequential conformational changes of the channel protein.

The squid model does not simply scale to body temperature: raise its temperature towards 37 °C and the spike first narrows, then fails altogether, because the gates speed up while the membrane's charging time does not: inactivation and potassium now outrun the sodium current before it can charge the capacitance. Mammalian channels solve this with different kinetics, not with a different temperature coefficient — which is why a separate parameter set is needed rather than a warmer squid.
