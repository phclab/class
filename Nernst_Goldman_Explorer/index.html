Nernst equation: equilibrium potential of one ion

Eion = RTzF ln [ion]o[ion]i

Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation: membrane potential from several ions

Vm = RTF ln PK[K⁺]o + PNa[Na⁺]o + PCl[Cl⁻]i PK[K⁺]i + PNa[Na⁺]i + PCl[Cl⁻]o
R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹F = 96485 C·mol⁻¹

Nernst: one ion's balance point

A concentration gradient drives an ion toward the side where it is scarcer, and the membrane voltage pushes back on its charge. The voltage at which the two forces cancel is that ion's equilibrium potential. At 37 °C for a monovalent ion, RT/F ≈ 26.7 mV, or about 61.5 mV per tenfold concentration ratio in base-10 logs.

Cl⁻ carries a negative charge (z = −1), so its ratio points the opposite way to a cation's. Ca²⁺ is divalent (z = +2), so the same concentration ratio gives half the voltage.

Goldman: the most permeable ion wins

The membrane lets several ions through at once, so Vm lands between their equilibrium potentials, weighted toward the most permeable one. At rest K⁺ channels dominate and Vm sits near EK. At the peak of an action potential Na⁺ permeability jumps twentyfold or more and Vm swings toward ENa.

Cl⁻ appears with inside and outside swapped because its charge is opposite to that of the cations.

Assumptions

  • Passive diffusion only, with zero net current (steady state).
  • A uniform electric field across the membrane (constant-field assumption); ions cross independently.
  • Permeabilities are relative; only their ratios matter, so PK = 1 is the usual reference.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the gradients, but its small direct electrogenic contribution is not included.
