Acetylcholine

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Fast at muscle · Modulatory in brain

Acetylcholine was the first transmitter ever identified, and it is the one that breaks the usual rules. It is not taken back up intact: an enzyme sitting in the cleft cuts it apart within a millisecond, and only the choline is recycled. The same molecule drives muscle with millisecond precision and sets attention and arousal over minutes.

Acetylcholinesterase is among the fastest enzymes known, turning over close to the limit set by diffusion. That speed is what lets a motor nerve fire at 50 hertz without the endplate saturating.

The five steps at a cholinergic synapse

  1. Synthesis. Choline acetyltransferase joins choline to acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm. The neuron cannot make choline, so the high-affinity transporter CHT1 that recaptures it sets the ceiling on how much transmitter exists.
  2. Loading. VAChT exchanges two protons for each acetylcholine molecule, filling vesicles that hold a few thousand molecules each.
  3. Release. At the human neuromuscular junction one action potential releases some twenty to thirty vesicles into an elaborately folded cleft; the folds concentrate Na⁺ channels, and the resulting safety factor is large enough that transmission never normally fails.
  4. Receptor binding. Nicotinic receptors are cation channels that open in well under a millisecond. Muscarinic receptors are G-protein coupled and act over hundreds of milliseconds, which is why the same molecule can be a command or a mood.
  5. Clearance. Acetylcholinesterase, anchored in the basal lamina of the cleft, hydrolyses acetylcholine into choline and acetate. CHT1 then pulls the choline back into the terminal to be used again.

When it goes wrong

  • Myasthenia gravis. Antibodies against the muscle nicotinic receptor, or against MuSK, remove the safety factor; weakness worsens with use and, in the receptor-antibody form, improves with cholinesterase inhibitors — the MuSK form often does not.
  • Lambert–Eaton syndrome. Antibodies against presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels cut release instead, so strength briefly improves with repeated effort — the mirror image of myasthenia.
  • Alzheimer's disease. Early loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons; cholinesterase inhibitors partly compensate for what remains.
  • Botulism and organophosphate poisoning. Too little release, or too much transmitter left in the cleft — the two ends of the same axis.
