GABA

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Fast · Inhibitory

GABA is made from glutamate by removing one carboxyl group — the brain builds its brake out of its accelerator. About one in five cortical neurons is GABAergic, and without them the excitatory network seizes within seconds.

GAD needs vitamin B6 as a cofactor. This is why severe B6 deficiency, or isoniazid poisoning which depletes it, causes seizures that need B6 itself — phenytoin does not touch them, though benzodiazepines help.

The five steps at a GABA synapse

  1. Synthesis. Glutamic acid decarboxylase removes a carboxyl group from glutamate. GAD67 keeps the cytoplasmic pool filled; GAD65 sits on vesicles and supplies extra GABA during heavy firing.
  2. Loading. VGAT, the same transporter used by glycinergic neurons, concentrates GABA into vesicles using the proton gradient.
  3. Release. The same Ca²⁺-triggered SNARE machinery as at excitatory synapses. Many GABAergic interneurons fire fast and release at high rates, so their terminals hold large, quickly recycled vesicle pools.
  4. Receptor binding. GABAA receptors open a chloride channel within a millisecond. GABAB receptors act through G proteins, opening K⁺ channels and closing Ca²⁺ channels over hundreds of milliseconds.
  5. Clearance. GAT-1 on neurons and GAT-3 on astrocytes recover GABA; what is not reused is degraded by GABA transaminase into succinic semialdehyde and fed into the Krebs cycle.

Inhibition depends on the chloride gradient

Why the same channel can excite

A GABAA receptor does not hyperpolarise the cell; it clamps the membrane towards the chloride equilibrium potential. In a mature neuron KCC2 rides the K⁺ gradient to carry Cl⁻ out, ECl sits near −70 mV, and opening the channel pulls the cell away from threshold. Even when ECl equals the resting potential, the extra conductance still shunts incoming excitatory current.

When the gradient reverses

Immature neurons express NKCC1 instead and accumulate Cl⁻, so GABA depolarises them and helps drive early network activity, at least in slices. The same reversal reappears in injured or epileptic tissue when KCC2 is downregulated. That, together with the internalisation of synaptic GABAA receptors during a long seizure, is why benzodiazepines can fail in status epilepticus.

The Nernst equation for Cl⁻ is doing the work here: change the concentration ratio and the sign of the response changes with it.

When it goes wrong

  • Epilepsy. Losing inhibitory interneurons, or losing KCC2, tips the local balance towards runaway excitation.
  • Stiff-person syndrome. Antibodies against GAD65 are best read as a marker: the antigen is intracellular and their pathogenicity is still argued. Glycine receptor antibodies, found in some patients and in the PERM variant, target a surface protein and are far more likely to do the damage themselves.
  • Anxiety disorders. Reduced GABAergic tone in amygdala circuits; benzodiazepines work fast because they amplify a signal already there.
  • Withdrawal. After chronic alcohol or benzodiazepine exposure the receptor population adapts; stopping abruptly leaves excitation unopposed, causing tremor, delirium and seizures.
