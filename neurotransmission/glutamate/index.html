Glutamate

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Fast · Excitatory

Glutamate carries almost all the excitatory traffic in the central nervous system. It is also an ordinary metabolite present everywhere in the cell, so the neuron cannot mark a synapse as active by making glutamate — only by putting it in the right place at the right moment, and taking it away fast.

Resting glutamate in the cleft is held near 25 nanomolar. Inside a vesicle it is at least 100 millimolar — a gradient of four million-fold or more. Filling the vesicles is cheap; what the synapse really pays for is pumping back the ions its receptors let in.

The five steps at a glutamate synapse

  1. Synthesis. Glutaminase converts glutamine, supplied by astrocytes, into glutamate. Glutamate cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so its carbon has to be recycled on the spot; glutamine is the form in which the astrocyte hands it back.
  2. Loading. VGLUT1–3 pack a few thousand molecules into each vesicle, driven mainly by the electrical component of the proton gradient. VGLUT1 dominates cortex and hippocampus, VGLUT2 thalamus and brainstem.
  3. Release. Ca²⁺ entering through P/Q- and N-type channels within nanometres of the docked vesicle triggers fusion. The cleft concentration jumps to about 1 millimolar for well under a millisecond.
  4. Receptor binding. AMPA receptors open immediately and carry the fast excitatory current. NMDA receptors need glutamate and depolarisation to expel their Mg²⁺ block, so they report coincidence rather than arrival.
  5. Clearance. EAAT2 on astrocytes removes most of the glutamate; glutamine synthetase converts it to glutamine and returns it. No enzyme destroys glutamate in the cleft — geometry and transporters do all the work.

A synapse with NMDA receptors but no AMPA receptors is electrically silent at rest. Making it audible by inserting AMPA receptors is one of the clearest physical pictures of learning we have.

Coincidence detection and plasticity

Why the Mg²⁺ block matters

At rest the NMDA channel is plugged by Mg²⁺. Glutamate alone cannot clear it. Only when the postsynaptic cell is already depolarised — because other inputs fired, or because the cell spiked — does the block leave and Ca²⁺ flow in. The receptor therefore fires only when presynaptic activity and postsynaptic activity coincide, which is Hebb's rule written in protein.

What the Ca²⁺ decides

A large, brief Ca²⁺ rise activates CaMKII and adds AMPA receptors: long-term potentiation. A small, prolonged rise favours phosphatases such as calcineurin and removes them: long-term depression. The same receptor, the same ion, and the amplitude decides the direction.

When it goes wrong

  • Excitotoxicity. In stroke or trauma, energy failure stops the transporters and reverses them. Glutamate accumulates, NMDA receptors stay open, Ca²⁺ floods in and kills the cell. This is why clearance, not release, is the rate-limiting safety step.
  • Epilepsy. Runaway recurrent excitation in cortex and hippocampus; several anti-epileptics reduce glutamate release or block AMPA receptors.
  • Schizophrenia. The NMDA hypofunction hypothesis holds that reduced NMDA signalling on inhibitory interneurons disinhibits pyramidal cells, matching what PCP and ketamine do.
  • Chronic pain. NMDA-dependent plasticity in the dorsal horn produces central sensitisation, which is why ketamine helps some neuropathic pain.
