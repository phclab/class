Glycine

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Fast · Inhibitory

Glycine is the simplest amino acid and it does two opposite jobs. In the spinal cord and brainstem it opens chloride channels and inhibits; everywhere else it occupies the NMDA receptor's co-agonist site, which must be filled — by glycine or by D-serine — before glutamate can open the channel at all.

Glycine and GABA share one vesicular transporter, so many spinal interneurons load both into the same vesicle and release them together. The postsynaptic cell decides which one matters by which receptors it puts in the membrane.

The five steps at a glycinergic synapse

  1. Synthesis. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase makes glycine from serine. Unlike GABA, glycine is also a general metabolite and a building block of proteins, so the transmitter pool is defined by where it is stored, not by its chemistry.
  2. Loading. VIAAT, the same vesicular transporter GABAergic neurons use, concentrates glycine into vesicles.
  3. Release. The usual Ca²⁺-triggered SNARE machinery. Renshaw cells and other spinal interneurons fire fast, so recurrent inhibition arrives back at the motor neuron within a few milliseconds of its own spike.
  4. Receptor binding. Glycine receptors are pentameric chloride channels built from α1–α3 and β subunits (other mammals have a fourth α gene; in humans it is a pseudogene), clustered opposite the terminal by gephyrin. The response looks like a GABAA response and follows the same chloride rules.
  5. Clearance. GlyT2 on the neuron recovers glycine to refill vesicles; GlyT1 on astrocytes keeps the ambient level low, which is how it controls the NMDA co-agonist site at nearby excitatory synapses.

One molecule, two opposite roles

The brake

In the spinal cord, glycine carries the reciprocal inhibition that stops an antagonist muscle contracting while its partner shortens, and the recurrent inhibition that limits motor neuron firing. Remove it — with strychnine, or with tetanus toxin, which stops release from inhibitory terminals — and every stimulus produces a whole-body spasm.

The permit

At excitatory synapses glycine binds a separate site on the NMDA receptor's GluN1 subunit. Glutamate alone does nothing there; the channel needs both. In the forebrain D-serine, made by astrocytes and neurons, occupies most of that site, while glycine dominates in the brainstem and spinal cord.

When it goes wrong

  • Hyperekplexia. Mutations in GLRA1, GLRB or GlyT2 leave too little glycinergic inhibition, so newborns stiffen and startle violently at a touch or a sound. Benzodiazepines help by substituting GABAergic inhibition.
  • Glycine encephalopathy. A failure of the glycine cleavage system lets glycine accumulate; the damage comes largely from over-activating NMDA receptors through the co-agonist site, not from too much inhibition.
  • Chronic inflammatory pain. Prostaglandin E₂ acts on EP2 receptors, and the PKA that follows phosphorylates the α3 glycine receptor in the dorsal horn and cuts its current, removing inhibition exactly where pain signals enter.
  • Tetanus and strychnine poisoning. Two different ways to delete the same brake, both producing rigidity that spares awareness.
