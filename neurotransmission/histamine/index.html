Histamine

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Slow · Modulatory

Every histamine neuron in the brain sits in one nucleus of the posterior hypothalamus, fires during waking and falls silent in sleep. It is also the one monoamine with no reuptake transporter: once released, a general organic cation transporter carries it into neighbouring cells, where it is methylated away.

In the brain histamine is methylated by HNMT inside neighbouring cells. Diamine oxidase, the other route, works in the gut and other peripheral tissues but not centrally — one reason drugs behave so differently either side of the blood-brain barrier.

The five steps at a histaminergic synapse

  1. Synthesis. Histidine decarboxylase removes the carboxyl group from histidine in one step. Mast cells use the same enzyme, which is why the peripheral and central stories share a molecule but nothing else.
  2. Loading. VMAT2, the transporter shared with dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin.
  3. Release. Tuberomammillary axons reach almost the whole brain and release from varicosities. Firing is highest in active waking, lower in quiet waking, and stops in both slow-wave and REM sleep.
  4. Receptor binding. All four receptors are G-protein coupled. H1 and H2 depolarise their targets and hold the cortex and thalamus in the waking state; H3 sits on the histamine terminal itself and turns release down.
  5. Clearance. No transporter takes histamine back into the neuron. Organic cation transporters carry it into surrounding cells, where histamine N-methyltransferase methylates it and MAO-B handles the product.

Why an allergy tablet makes you sleepy

The same receptor, two places

H1 receptors in the skin and airways produce itch, wheal and bronchoconstriction. The same H1 receptors on thalamic and cortical neurons keep them depolarised and awake. A first-generation antihistamine such as diphenhydramine crosses the blood-brain barrier and blocks both, so it treats the rash and takes out the arousal system with it.

Keeping the drug out

Cetirizine and fexofenadine carry charge at physiological pH, and they, loratadine and desloratadine are all substrates of P-glycoprotein at the blood-brain barrier, which the sedating antihistamines are not. Either route keeps central occupancy low. The same logic runs in reverse for pitolisant, an H3 inverse agonist used in narcolepsy, which must get in to raise histamine release.

When it goes wrong

  • Narcolepsy. The primary lesion is loss of orexin neurons, but the histamine system is one of the arousal circuits they drive, and H3 inverse agonists exploit what remains.
  • Sedation from medicines. Antihistamines, many antipsychotics and several antidepressants share H1 blockade; it is the most common reason a prescribed drug makes someone sleepy.
  • Anaphylaxis. A peripheral story: mast cell histamine acting on H1 in vessels and airways. Adrenaline, not an antihistamine, is the treatment.
  • Itch. Histaminergic itch responds to antihistamines; the chronic itch of cholestasis or neuropathy usually does not, because it runs on different mediators.
