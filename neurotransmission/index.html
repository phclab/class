Neurotransmission

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Almost every chemical synapse runs the same five steps: make the transmitter, load it into vesicles, release it, let it bind receptors, then clear it away. What changes from one transmitter to the next is which enzymes, which receptors, and which drugs can reach in and change the outcome.

The five steps, in every synapse

  1. Synthesis. Enzymes in the terminal (or in the cell body, for peptides) build the transmitter from a precursor the neuron takes up from blood or glia.
  2. Vesicular loading. A vesicular transporter uses the proton gradient across the vesicle membrane to concentrate the transmitter inside.
  3. Release. An action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ triggers SNARE-mediated fusion within a fraction of a millisecond.
  4. Receptor binding. Ionotropic receptors open a channel in under a millisecond; metabotropic receptors start a G-protein cascade that lasts hundreds of milliseconds to minutes.
  5. Clearance. Reuptake transporters, enzymatic breakdown, or simple diffusion end the signal and recycle the building blocks.

Almost every psychoactive drug, and a large share of neurological medicines, acts on one of these five steps in one transmitter system. The main exceptions are drugs that act directly on voltage-gated channels, and a few with intracellular targets such as lithium.
