Neuropeptides

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Slow · Modulatory · Co-released

A peptide cannot be finished in the terminal. Terminals do translate protein locally, but the cutting and tailoring a peptide needs happen only in the secretory pathway. It is transcribed, cut out of a larger precursor and packed into dense-core vesicles in the cell body, then carried down the axon — a journey of hours to days. Nothing recaptures it, so every molecule released is spent. That budget shapes everything about how peptides are used.

Dense-core vesicles sit away from the active zone, so they need calcium to rise throughout the terminal rather than in a nanodomain next to a channel. A single spike will not do it; a burst will.

The five steps, rewritten for peptides

  1. Synthesis. A gene is transcribed into a prepropeptide; signal peptidases cut it in the endoplasmic reticulum, and prohormone convertases finish the job in the Golgi and inside the young granule, where the acid switches them on. One precursor often yields several active peptides — proopiomelanocortin gives β-endorphin, ACTH and α-MSH.
  2. Loading and transport. The peptides are packed into dense-core vesicles at the Golgi and moved down the axon by kinesin. Refilling a terminal therefore takes hours, not seconds.
  3. Release. Only when calcium rises broadly, which in practice means high-frequency or bursting activity. Release sites are outside the active zone, so the peptide starts its journey already some distance from any receptor.
  4. Receptor binding. Almost every peptide receptor is a GPCR with nanomolar affinity, which is what lets a peptide work after diffusing micrometres and being diluted. Effects last seconds to minutes.
  5. Clearance. Extracellular peptidases such as neprilysin and aminopeptidases cut the peptide apart. There is no reuptake and no recycling: the terminal must wait for the next delivery from the cell body.

Co-release: two messages from one terminal

Different thresholds

Most peptidergic neurons also release a classical transmitter. At low firing rates only the small clear vesicles at the active zone fuse, so the postsynaptic cell gets the fast message alone. As firing intensifies the dense-core vesicles join in, adding a slower, wider-reaching signal. The same axon says something different depending on how hard it is working.

Examples worth knowing

Substance P with glutamate in nociceptive afferents; enkephalin with GABA in the striatum; NPY with noradrenaline in sympathetic terminals; CGRP with acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction. In each case the peptide is the part that only appears under load.

When it goes wrong

  • Narcolepsy type 1. Loss of about ninety per cent of the fifty to eighty thousand orexin neurons, almost certainly autoimmune, leaving sleep and wake boundaries unstable.
  • Migraine. CGRP release from trigeminal fibres sensitises and dilates; blocking it prevents attacks without constricting vessels, unlike the triptans.
  • Opioid tolerance and dependence. Receptor desensitisation and circuit adaptation to a signal the brain normally releases only in bursts.
  • Cachexia and obesity. The hypothalamic peptides — NPY, AgRP, α-MSH — are where appetite is actually computed, and where several new drugs now act.
