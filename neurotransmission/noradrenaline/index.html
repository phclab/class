Noradrenaline

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Slow · Modulatory

Noradrenaline is dopamine with one more hydroxyl group, and the enzyme that adds it sits inside the vesicle — so this is the one transmitter whose final synthetic step happens after loading. Fifteen to twenty thousand neurons per side in the locus coeruleus then broadcast it to the entire forebrain and cerebellum.

At a glance

Because dopamine β-hydroxylase is a vesicular enzyme, anything that empties vesicles releases a mixture, and a cell that lacks the enzyme simply releases dopamine instead — which is exactly what happens in inherited dopamine β-hydroxylase deficiency.

The five steps at a noradrenergic synapse

  1. Synthesis. Tyrosine → L-DOPA → dopamine in the cytoplasm, exactly as in a dopamine neuron. Dopamine β-hydroxylase then converts dopamine to noradrenaline inside the vesicle, after VMAT2 has carried it in.
  2. Loading. VMAT2 again. In the adrenal medulla one further enzyme, PNMT, makes adrenaline in the cytosol and it is loaded back — the same chain stopped one step later.
  3. Release. Locus coeruleus neurons fire tonically at one to three hertz and burst to salient events. Their axons are unmyelinated, branched across the whole cortex, and release mostly from varicosities.
  4. Receptor binding. All adrenoceptors are G-protein coupled: α1 excites through Gq, α2 inhibits through Gi and acts as the autoreceptor, β receptors raise cAMP.
  5. Clearance. NET recaptures most of it. MAO-A inside the terminal and COMT in neighbouring cells break down the rest; in the periphery, uptake into smooth muscle and circulating clearance matter as much.

What the locus coeruleus is doing

Tonic and phasic

Low tonic firing goes with drowsiness, moderate tonic firing with focused attention, and high tonic firing with distractible, anxious scanning — an inverted U, like dopamine in prefrontal cortex. Phasic bursts are locked to task-relevant surprises and briefly sharpen the cortex that receives them.

Gain, not content

Noradrenaline rarely drives a cell by itself. It raises the contrast between strong and weak inputs, favours the strongest, and promotes plasticity where it lands. That is why it shows up in arousal, in memory for emotional events, and in the intrusive memories of post-traumatic stress disorder.

Adrenaline and noradrenaline act on the same receptors with different preferences: noradrenaline is relatively stronger at α, adrenaline at β2. That single difference is most of the pharmacology of the sympathetic system.

When it goes wrong

  • Post-traumatic stress disorder. Excess noradrenergic signalling during and after a threat strengthens consolidation; prazosin at night reduces nightmares in some patients.
  • ADHD. On the leading account, too little catecholamine signalling in prefrontal cortex; both noradrenergic and dopaminergic drugs help, again on an inverted-U dose-response.
  • Orthostatic hypotension. Loss of peripheral noradrenergic function, in pure autonomic failure or in dopamine β-hydroxylase deficiency, where the patient releases dopamine instead.
  • Locus coeruleus degeneration. Among the earliest pathology in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, which is one reason arousal and sleep change years before the diagnosis.
