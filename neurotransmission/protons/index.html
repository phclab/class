Protons

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Fast · Excitatory · Co-released

The proton follows the five steps as faithfully as glutamate does. It is concentrated inside the vesicle, released by the same Ca²⁺-triggered fusion, bound by receptors built to detect it, and cleared by the buffers around it. The one step it skips is synthesis — nothing has to make a proton.

The acid inside the vesicle is not a side effect of storage: it is the energy source for storage. VAChT and VMAT2 spend this gradient directly, two protons per molecule, which is why the proton arrives in the cleft alongside whatever it paid for. VGLUT is the exception: it runs mostly on the voltage across the vesicle membrane, as the glutamate page says.

The five steps at an acidifying synapse

  1. Synthesis. There is none. Protons come from ordinary metabolism and are everywhere already; the synapse only has to move them.
  2. Loading. A V-type ATPase spends ATP to pump protons in: glutamatergic vesicles settle near pH 5.6, GABAergic ones nearer 6.4. The transporters then trade that acid away — two protons for each acetylcholine, two for each monoamine. Glutamate is the odd one out: VGLUT leans on the voltage across the vesicle membrane rather than on the pH gradient, and whether it moves protons at all is unresolved. Almost none of the protons inside are free: at pH 5.6 a vesicle holds less than one free H⁺, and what leaves is the thousand or so sitting on the buffers.
  3. Release. The same Ca²⁺-triggered SNARE fusion. At ribbon and calyceal synapses, where the cleft is enclosed, release acidifies it by a few tenths of a pH unit over tens of milliseconds. At two conventional terminals imaged with pH probes the cleft turned alkaline instead, so how general the acid transient is remains unsettled.
  4. Receptor binding. Acid-sensing ion channels open: ligand-gated channels whose ligand is H⁺, carrying mostly sodium, with the ASIC1a homomer passing some calcium too, and all of them desensitising within a second or two of the acid arriving. The same acid transient also inhibits NMDA receptors and some Ca²⁺ channels, so one release event both excites and damps, on different targets.
  5. Clearance. No transporter carries the proton back. Bicarbonate and carbonic anhydrase absorb it, diffusion out of the cleft dilutes it, and Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers on the surrounding membranes restore the resting pH.

A transmitter, or the exhaust of loading a transmitter?

The case against

The proton is released whether or not it is wanted. Nothing regulates it independently of the transmitter it was traded for, there is no proton-specific vesicle, and the acidification is unavoidable given how vesicles are filled. On that reading it is exhaust, not a message.

The case for

There are receptors tuned to it, and ASIC1a sits in dendritic spines at the postsynaptic density, opposite the release sites. Blocking it removes a measurable component of the response — five to ten per cent of the postsynaptic current in amygdala and accumbens. A signal does not have to be purpose-built to be used: evolution reads whatever is reliably there.

Both statements are true at once, which is why the label is still argued. It is a good example for students of how the definition of a transmitter is a matter of criteria rather than of substance.

Where it matters

The same test in hippocampus came out negative — blocking ASIC1a there left long-term potentiation and spatial memory intact — so how far the mechanism reaches is argued.

There is still no clinical drug aimed at ASICs. The channels are attractive targets for stroke and for pain, and nothing has yet made it through.

When it goes wrong

  • Ischaemic acidosis. When blood flow stops, lactic acid drops tissue pH by a full unit or more. ASIC1a opens for as long as the acid lasts and admits Ca²⁺, adding an injury route that glutamate blockers do not touch.
  • Inflammatory and ischaemic pain. ASIC3 in sensory endings reports the acid directly, which is why angina and claudication hurt in the way they do.
  • Panic and CO₂ sensitivity. Inhaling carbon dioxide provokes panic far more readily in people with panic disorder. In mice the fear response needs ASIC1a in amygdala and bed nucleus; in humans the link is still inference, and people who have lost both amygdalae can still be panicked by CO₂.
  • Seizure termination. Seizures acidify tissue, and that acid appears to help end them — one reason breathing quickly, which lowers CO₂ and raises pH, can prolong an absence seizure.
