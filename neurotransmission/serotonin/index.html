Serotonin

by Po-Han Chiang @ NYCU

Slow · Modulatory

A couple of hundred thousand neurons in the raphe nuclei supply the entire human brain with serotonin, and they act through fourteen different receptors — one of which is an ion channel and the rest G-protein coupled. That is why a single drug that raises serotonin can calm, nauseate, and shift mood on three different timescales.

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid and TPH2 is not saturated at normal levels, so brain serotonin genuinely depends on what arrives from the diet — the basis of tryptophan depletion experiments.

The five steps at a serotonergic synapse

  1. Synthesis. Tryptophan hydroxylase 2 makes 5-hydroxytryptophan; the same decarboxylase that finishes dopamine then finishes serotonin. TPH1, the peripheral form, supplies the gut, which holds most of the body's serotonin.
  2. Loading. VMAT2, shared with the other monoamines, packs serotonin into vesicles.
  3. Release. Raphe axons are thin, enormously branched and studded with varicosities, so release is diffuse: one neuron can influence a whole cortical area at once.
  4. Receptor binding. Thirteen of the fourteen receptors are G-protein coupled; only 5-HT3 is an ion channel. Somatodendritic 5-HT1A autoreceptors in the raphe slow the cell down as soon as serotonin rises.
  5. Clearance. SERT takes serotonin back; MAO-A converts what is not reused into 5-HIAA, the metabolite measured in cerebrospinal fluid.

Why antidepressants take weeks

The first dose

An SSRI blocks SERT within hours, and serotonin rises everywhere — including on the 5-HT1A autoreceptors of the raphe neurons themselves. Those receptors hyperpolarise the cell and cut firing, so more transmitter in the cleft is offset by fewer spikes arriving. Net output barely changes, while the side effects, mediated by 5-HT3 in the gut and 5-HT2A elsewhere, appear immediately.

After a few weeks

Sustained stimulation desensitises those autoreceptors. Firing recovers while the transporter stays blocked, so forebrain serotonin finally rises. Downstream changes — altered plasticity and, in animal work, hippocampal neurogenesis — follow on the same timescale as the clinical response. On this account the delay is a property of the circuit rather than of the drug reaching its target, though the improvement many patients report within a fortnight keeps the argument open.

This also explains why pindolol, a β-blocker that acts as a weak partial agonist and so functionally blocks the 5-HT1A autoreceptor, was tested as a way to speed antidepressant onset: it shortened the delay in some trials without raising the final response rate.

When it goes wrong

  • Serotonin syndrome. Two serotonergic drugs together — classically an SSRI with an MAO inhibitor, tramadol or linezolid — produce agitation, clonus, hyperreflexia and fever. Hyperreflexia and clonus, worse in the legs and building over hours, distinguish it from the lead-pipe rigidity and hyporeflexia of neuroleptic malignant syndrome, which builds over days.
  • Depression. Not a simple serotonin deficiency: depleting tryptophan does not make healthy people depressed, and the delay to response shows the transmitter is a lever on the system, not the fault itself.
  • Migraine. Serotonin falls between attacks and rises during them; triptans work at the 5-HT1B/1D receptors on vessels and trigeminal terminals.
  • Gut symptoms. Most of the body's serotonin is made by enterochromaffin cells, which is why SSRIs so often cause nausea and altered bowel habit at the start.
